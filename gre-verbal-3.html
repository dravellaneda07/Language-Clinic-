Questions 1–6: Text Completion

For each question, select one entry for each blank from the corresponding column of choices. Fill all blanks in the way that best completes the text.
1Mathematicians have a distinctive sense of beauty: they strive to present their ideas and results in a clear and compelling fashion, dictated by ______ as well as by logic.
The sentence opens with mathematicians’ “sense of beauty,” and the part after the colon explains it. The blank must name something beauty-related that sits alongside logic: aesthetics. Intellect and methodologies repeat the idea of logic rather than adding beauty. Caprice (whim) contradicts “clear and compelling.”
2By recognizing commonalities among all the major political parties and by promoting a collaborative decision-making process, the prime minister has made good on his promise to cultivate a leadership style that emphasizes ______.
The actions in the first half, finding common ground and promoting collaboration, are examples of the leadership style. The word that summarizes them is cooperation. Ideology and differentiation point toward what divides parties, the opposite of the examples. Growth and politics are unrelated to the examples given.
3An innovation of the eighteenth-century cookbook writer Mary Cole was that in her work she (i) ______ the earlier books from which her recipes were drawn. Even in those numerous instances in which she had collated into a single version, which she could have called her own, the recipes of several earlier writers, she (ii) ______ them.
Blank (i)
Blank (ii)
Start with Blank (ii). “Even … which she could have called her own” signals that she did the opposite of claiming the recipes: she took pains to cite the earlier writers. The second sentence is an extreme case of the first, so Blank (i) must mean the same thing: acknowledged. Preserving or enhancing earlier books would not be an “innovation” in giving credit.
4Well known for his combative style, the author is even more (i) ______ than usual in his recent book: in it, he attempts to (ii) ______ his intellectual opponents with a barrage of ill-supported assertions.
Blank (i)
Blank (ii)
“Even more ______ than usual” must build on “combative,” so Blank (i) is contentious (quarrelsome). Unctuous (overly flattering) and reticent (reserved) are not combative. The colon introduces an example of that aggressive style: firing a “barrage” of claims at opponents is an attempt to vanquish (defeat) them, not to placate or merely acknowledge them.
5The lizards snapped up insects that are so (i) ______ that other potential predators avoid them. Among the lizards’ prey were some beetles that they initially (ii) ______ because the insects were spraying their hot, irritant defense chemical at the time. Yet even these produced no apparent ill effects, since the lizards, having eaten, proceeded on their way (iii) ______ enough.
Blank (i)
Blank (ii)
Blank (iii)
Predators avoid the insects, so they must be harmful: noxious (Blank i). Rare or nutritious insects would not be avoided. “Because” the beetles were spraying an irritant, the lizards at first turned them down: rejected (Blank ii). Finally, “no apparent ill effects” means the lizards walked away unbothered: nonchalantly (Blank iii). Erratically or laboriously would suggest they were harmed.
6Bureaucrats tend to (i) ______. So it is surprising that the European Commission is proposing to hand back some of its antitrust powers to national governments. Such a willingness to (ii) ______ power is quite (iii) ______. Perhaps the commission, so often a byword for meddling, bungling, and even corruption, is starting to put its house in order following the forced resignation of the previous lot of commissioners last year.
Blank (i)
Blank (ii)
Blank (iii)
Giving up power is “surprising,” so bureaucrats normally do the opposite: guard their authority jealously (Blank i). Handing powers back to national governments means to devolve (delegate) power (Blank ii); misuse and appropriate (take) do not describe giving it away. The last sentence is hopeful (“put its house in order”), so the move is encouraging (Blank iii). It cannot be predictable, because the passage calls it surprising.

Questions 7–10: Reading Comprehension

For each question, select one answer choice.
Passage for Questions 7–10

During the 1920s, most advocates of scientific management, Frederick Taylor’s method for maximizing workers’ productivity by rigorously routinizing their jobs, opposed the five-day workweek. Although scientific managers conceded that reducing hours might provide an incentive to workers, in practice they more often used pay differentials to encourage higher productivity. Those reformers who wished to embrace both scientific management and reduced hours had to make a largely negative case, portraying the latter as an antidote to the rigors of the former.

In contrast to the scientific managers, Henry Ford claimed that shorter hours led to greater productivity and profits. However, few employers matched either Ford’s vision or his specific interest in mass marketing a product—automobiles—that required leisure for its use, and few unions succeeded in securing shorter hours through bargaining. At its 1928 convention, the American Federation of Labor (AFL) boasted of approximately 165,000 members working five-day, 40-hour weeks. But although this represented an increase of about 75,000 since 1926, about 70 percent of the total came from five extremely well-organized building trades’ unions.

7The passage is primarily concerned with discussing which of the following?
The passage describes who opposed and who supported the five-day workweek in the 1920s and how far it had actually spread. That is the status of a contested issue, D. A is wrong because the author lays out positions without weighing which is better. B and C go beyond the passage: it does not focus on benefits to workers or explain why labor and management disagreed. E is too narrow; unions appear only in the last part, and mostly to show their limited success.
8It can be inferred that the author of the passage mentions “automobiles” (second paragraph) primarily to suggest that
The author notes that automobiles are a product that “required leisure for its use.” Workers with more free time are more likely to buy cars, which hints at a business reason behind Ford’s support for shorter hours: C. A is not supported, because no profit comparison is made. B goes too far: the passage is about consumers in general, not Ford’s own employees. D and E are wrong because the reference to automobiles has nothing to do with unions.
9It can be inferred that the author of the passage would probably agree with which of the following claims about the boast referred to in the second paragraph (the AFL’s 1928 convention)?
Right after the boast, the author adds “But …” and points out that about 70 percent of those workers came from just five building trades’ unions. The large total could make it seem that many unions had won the shorter week, when in fact only a few had: B. The author does not question the numbers themselves, so A, C, and E are wrong. D is wrong because the passage calls those unions “extremely well-organized,” which supports their success rather than doubting it.
10According to the passage, the “reformers” (first paragraph) claimed that
The reformers portrayed reduced hours as “an antidote to the rigors” of scientific management. An antidote counteracts harm, so they claimed shorter weeks could offset the toll of routinized jobs: B. C is tempting but wrong: the reformers wanted to embrace both ideas, so they argued the two could work together. A contradicts their position. D and E are claims about pay and productivity that the passage does not attribute to the reformers.

Questions 11–13: Sentence Equivalence

Select the two answer choices that, when used to complete the sentence, fit the meaning of the sentence as a whole and produce completed sentences that are alike in meaning.
11Although the compound is abundant in the environment at large, its presence in the air is not ______; only in the form of underwater sediment does it cause damage.
“Only in the form of underwater sediment does it cause damage” means the compound is harmless in the air. With “not” before the blank, the blank must mean harmful: deleterious and detrimental. Trivial and inconsequential are a synonym pair, but “not trivial” would mean the opposite of what the sentence needs.
12Although relying on much of the recent scholarship on the bison, Lott’s book is a distinctly ______ and even idiosyncratic contribution to the field.
“Although” contrasts the book’s reliance on others’ work with something new about it, and “even idiosyncratic” intensifies that newness. Original and innovative fit. Derivative and imitative are a synonym pair that says the opposite. Insightful and surprising each fit loosely but do not mean the same thing.
13Despite relying on the well-to-do for commissions, the portrait painter was no ______: he depicted the character of those he painted as he perceived it.
“Despite” depending on wealthy patrons, he painted them honestly instead of flattering them. A flatterer is a sycophant or an adulator. Egotist and braggart are a synonym pair but have nothing to do with flattering patrons. Hypocrite and coward can make some sense in the blank, but they don’t mean the same thing.

Questions 14–15: Reading Comprehension

For Question 14, consider each choice separately and select all that apply. For Question 15, select one answer choice.
Passage for Questions 14–15

Scientists formerly believed that the rocky planets—Earth, Mercury, Venus, and Mars—were created by the rapid gravitational collapse of a dust cloud, a deflation giving rise to a dense orb. That view was challenged in the 1960s, when studies of Moon craters revealed that these craters were caused by the impact of objects that were in great abundance about 4.5 billion years ago but whose number appeared to have quickly decreased shortly thereafter. This observation rejuvenated Otto Schmidt’s 1944 theory of accretion. According to this theory, cosmic dust gradually lumped into ever-larger conglomerates: particulates, gravel, small and then larger balls, planetesimals (tiny planets), and, ultimately, planets. As the planetesimals became larger, their numbers decreased. Consequently, the number of collisions between planetesimals decreased.

14The passage provides evidence that Schmidt would be likely to DISAGREE with the theory presented in the first sentence over

Select all that apply.

The first theory says the planets formed by “rapid” collapse; Schmidt says dust “gradually” built up into planets. They disagree about how long formation took, so A is correct. B is wrong because the passage never says what either theory claimed about the Moon’s craters; the crater studies came later. C is wrong because both theories start with cosmic dust, so they agree on that point.
15Which of the following best describes the “observation” referred to in the passage (“This observation rejuvenated Otto Schmidt’s 1944 theory”)?
“This observation” refers back to the previous sentence: the objects striking the Moon were very numerous about 4.5 billion years ago, and then their number dropped quickly. That is D. A is the older theory, and C is Schmidt’s theory, not the observation. B leaves out the key point about the pattern of impacts over time. E is a conclusion drawn from Schmidt’s theory, not what the crater studies observed.
